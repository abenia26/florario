Which flowers to send

In grief, flowers say what is hard to put into words. These are the most common:

  • White lily: peace and purity. It is the most widespread sympathy flower in Latin America and the US.
  • White roses: respect and remembrance; dark red roses express deep love for the person who has gone.
  • Chrysanthemums: in Spain, France and Italy they are the flower of remembrance par excellence.
  • White carnations: remembrance and affection; very long-lasting.
  • Gladioli: strength and integrity; widely used in wreaths because of their height.
  • White orchids: a plant the family can keep for a long time.

White is the most common color, but it isn’t compulsory: soft tones, such as lilac, pale pink or green, are just as appropriate, and if the person who died had a favorite flower or color, choosing it is a beautiful tribute.

Wreath, arrangement, bouquet or plant

  • Wreath: for the funeral home or the ceremony. It is usually sent by a group (coworkers, friends, a company) and has a ribbon with the sender’s name.
  • Arrangement or floral cushion: more discreet than a wreath, also for the funeral home.
  • Bouquet: to give to the family at home, in person or by delivery.
  • Plant: an orchid, a peace lily or a small tree. It lasts and keeps the family company long after the funeral.

If you’re sending to a funeral home, include the full name of the deceased, the room and the times of the wake. Florists nearby usually know the logistics well.

What to write on the card

There’s no need to be eloquent; what matters is that it is sincere and brief. Some phrases:

  • “With deepest sympathy. With all our love.”
  • “In memory of [name], who always made us laugh.”
  • “We are with you. We’re here for anything you need.”
  • “With love and a huge hug for the whole family.”

Always sign with your name, and your family’s or company’s if appropriate, so the family knows who was there for them.

Customs by religion and culture

  • Catholic and Christian tradition in general: flowers at the wake, the mass and the cemetery are customary.
  • Jewish tradition: sending flowers isn’t customary. It is better to be there in person, bring food to the family during shiva or make a donation.
  • Muslim tradition: many families don’t send flowers either; ask first or choose another gesture.
  • Mexico: beyond the funeral, remembrance continues on the Day of the Dead, with cempasúchil on the altar.

If the family has asked for donations instead of flowers, respect their wishes.

Flowers after the funeral

The days after the burial are often the hardest: the visits stop and the house falls silent. A bouquet or plant a week or two later, or on the first anniversary, says “I’m still here” in a way the family remembers.
