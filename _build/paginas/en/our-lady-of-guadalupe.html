The story of the roses of Tepeyac

Catholic tradition says that between December 9 and 12, 1531 the Virgin appeared several times to Juan Diego, an Indigenous man who lived near Tepeyac hill, north of today’s Mexico City. She asked him to go to the bishop, Friar Juan de Zumárraga, so that he would build a church on that spot. The bishop didn’t believe him and asked for a sign.

On December 12, the Virgin told Juan Diego to climb to the top of the hill and gather flowers. There, on barren ground and in the middle of winter, he found Castilian roses: flowers that weren’t from the region and don’t bloom in central Mexico in December. He gathered them in his tilma, the cotton cloak he wore, and took them to the bishop. When he opened it, the roses fell to the ground and the image of Our Lady of Guadalupe appeared on the cloth, the same image venerated today in the Basilica.

That’s why roses aren’t just any decoration on this date: in the story they are the proof of the miracle. Bringing roses to the Virgin on December 12 is, for millions of people, repeating Juan Diego’s gesture.

How December 12 is celebrated

The celebration begins on the night of December 11. At midnight las mañanitas (a traditional birthday song) are sung to the Virgin at the Basilica of Guadalupe and in parishes across the country, often with mariachis. For days pilgrims arrive on foot, by bicycle or in organized groups from every state; many carry images of the Virgin and bunches of roses. In 2025, according to the Mexico City Government, some 13 million pilgrims came to the Basilica, one of the largest religious gatherings in the world.

Outside the capital, almost every neighborhood and village holds masses, processions, dances and decorated altars. And many homes set up a small altar with an image of the Virgin, candles and flowers, almost always roses.

Which flowers to bring to Our Lady of Guadalupe

  • Roses: they are the flower of the day. Red ones are the most common, but any color will do; what matters is the gesture.
  • A bouquet that’s easy to carry: if you’re going on pilgrimage, a medium-sized bouquet is best, with the stems wrapped in damp paper and plastic so it lasts hours on the road.
  • For the home altar: roses in a vase with clean water and, if you like, a poinsettia, which is everywhere at that time of year and is the other great Mexican flower of December.

Tips for buying the roses

December is a month of high demand in Mexico: between Poinsettia Day (December 8), Our Lady of Guadalupe (the 12th) and Christmas, roses go up in price and the showiest bouquets sell out in the markets near the Basilica. Buy a day or two ahead and keep the roses somewhere cool, with the stems freshly cut at an angle. If you’re taking them on the pilgrimage, choose roses with the bud still somewhat closed: they’ll open on the way and arrive at their best.

And if you can’t go to the Basilica, a single rose on your home altar or at your local parish carries the same meaning.

One date in a month full of flowers

Our Lady of Guadalupe caps a very floral December in Mexico: on the 8th comes National Poinsettia Day, for the Mexican flower that went around the world as the Christmas flower, and then come the posadas and Christmas, with their centerpieces. You’ll find all the month’s dates on the December flowers page and the rest of the year’s in the dates for giving flowers in Mexico.

Although the devotion is above all Mexican, Our Lady of Guadalupe is the patron saint of all the Americas, and December 12 is also celebrated with roses in Mexican and Latin American communities in the United States and other countries.
